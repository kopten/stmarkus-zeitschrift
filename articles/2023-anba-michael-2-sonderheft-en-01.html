Come to Me, all you who labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Matt 11,28 Bishop Michael, Abt of St. Anthony’s Monastery, Kröffelbach (1980-2023) and Bishop of the Diocese Southern Germany (2013-2023)

2 Foreword “That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled, of the Word of life…That which we have seen and heard declare we unto you, that ye also may have fellowship with us. (1 John 1-3) When we speak of His Grace, Anba Michael, we are speaking of a spiritual father, a celestial figure, and a heart open to all with genuine love for those who approach him. He was a wellspring of virtues, living as a monk and remaining so until his last day on earth. The episcopal rank did not change his simplicity, nor did it alter his way of life, and he never set himself apart or distinguished himself from others in any way. What's even more amazing is that neither illness nor old age changed his character or principles; he continued to serve despite his old age until his very last breath. He met the needs of his congregation and his monk

3 disciples without burdening those around him with his hardly endurable pains. He never imposed any burdens on anyone, even when he needed assistance in his last days. In the last few days when he could no longer walk unaided, he leaned against the wall to the best of his ability so as not to burden those around him. This made me realize that this is one of his principles, not to be a burden to others. Thus, our beloved father lived by serving everyone around him, praying for them to the very end. A few days before his departure, a lady came to him, crying, asking for prayers for her husband who had suddenly been admitted to the hospital. He listened to her, inquired about what had happened, prayed for her husband's healing, and reassured her. Indeed, just a few hours later, her husband's health rapidly improved, and he returned home.” (Brother A. A.)

6 Word of His Holiness Pope Tawadros II in bidding farewell to Anba Michael, the Bishop and Head of St. Anthony’s Monastery in Kröffelbach, Germany This week, we bid farewell to His Grace Anba Michael, one of the blessed fathers in the Holy Synod, whose passing we deeply regret. Having spent half of his life as a monk and a servant, he is considered one of the early priests to serve in Germany. He was sent there about 45 years ago by

7 Pope Shenouda, starting off with nothing but patience, prayer, hard work, effort, and love to establish the first Coptic monastery in Europe, the Monastery of St. Anthony. Later, Pope Shenouda consecrated the church of the monastery. As the first Coptic church in Europe to be built in the Egyptian Coptic style, both the church and the monastery became witnesses to Christ. A theological college there was soon established by Pope Shenouda. It developed to be an international beacon of theological education. After having served as a monk under the name of “Father Michael Al-Baramosy” for about 35 years, we appointed Anba Michael as a bishop of the Southern German Diocese and as the head of St. Anthony Monastery in Kröffelbach. In truth, for Father Michael not a day passed without service. He was a compassionate and approachable monk and very calm in temperament. Due to his continual trust in the Lord in all matters, he succeeded greatly in his service. The monastery grew into a major center for monks, priests, and a significant ministry. Anba Michael also founded a great number of churches throughout Germany: There are several small congregations scattered around Germany, consisting of 30 to 40 families. For these, he cared,

8 appointed priests, and founded churches. The Lord facilitated many things for him in his work alongside the monastery, making it a significant center for service there. With the help of all the fathers present in the monastery they managed to serve many churches. We remember him and his goodness and bid him farewell in the hope of the resurrection. All the fathers of the Holy Synod participated in this farewell. The funeral prayer took place in Germany on Wednesday, August 9, 2023. The fathers in Europe were tasked with conducting the funeral prayer, and many bishops, priests, and many who loved His Grace Anba Michael bid him farewell.

9 “Fear not! Jesus loves you and takes care of you.” By Bishop Michael In the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, one God. Amen. The Gospel of Luke 5:1-11 teaches us that the Lord Christ, praise be to Him, cares for each one of us. He has redeemed and saved us. Only Christ is our Redeemer and Savior. Besides, there is another important thing: He also cares about the small matters that concern us. "Yes, it's important to me. I came here specifically for him, for Peter and his brother Andrew and their friends James and John. The two brothers have

10 only one boat, and they have not caught any fish. Peter had given up and was washing his nets. The other two brothers also have only one boat, and they tried all night but caught no fish." "Is my very personal concern important to you, Lord? I have a problem at work. I'm having trouble with my studies. There's conflict in my family. I also have problems with my spiritual life. Are all these things important to you, Lord?" "Yes, they are very important to me. I stand by you and help you." "But how, Lord?" How wonderful is the God we Christians believe in! In other religions where people also believe in a God, it doesn't look like that. In those religions, God can be very distant in the heights of heaven. But our God is close to us and very kind. He doesn't hover far above the people in the clouds. No, He descended to us and took on flesh. He walks among us, shares our suffering, and helps us. He says that He didn't come to be served but to serve. Our God is kind. He loves His children and also others. “For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever

11 believes in him should not perish, but have everlasting life.” (John 3:16) Jesus came to the Sea of Galilee, where the fishermen stood with their boats. The Lord, praise be to Him, came to the shores of the sea because He cared about the poor fishermen. He knew they were sad because they hadn't caught any fish. However, the Lord first had to take care of the many people who had come from afar to hear His teachings. Since the people crowded around Him, the Lord got into Peter's boat and asked him to go out a bit onto the sea. Then He sat down and taught the people from the boat. After He finished speaking to the crowd, He turned to Peter, whom He had not forgotten, and said to him, "Go out to the deep water and let down your nets for a catch." Peter followed the Lord's instructions, even though, as an experienced fisherman, he knew that fish were not found in deep waters, and the chances of catching fish were greater at night than during the day. Humbly, Peter said to the Lord, "Master, we've worked hard all night and haven't caught anything. But because you say so, I will let down the nets." The men did as they were told and caught a large number of fish, so many that their nets began to break. (Luke 5:5-6)

12 Peter was amazed. He felt the strength, holiness, and almighty power of the Lord and said to Him, "Go away from me, Lord; I am a sinful man!" But Jesus comforted Peter and said, "Don't be afraid. From now on, you will be catching people." Jesus had come to help the poor fishermen who had been struggling all night without catching any fish. He came to help them and bring joy to them and their families. At the same time, Jesus cared for the people who had come to seek God. He made Peter a fisher of men with the miracle of the great catch, teaching Peter that success doesn't come solely from one's own efforts but rather from the grace of God. We should recognize this as well. Often, we think something has to work out because we have done certain things. Peter could have said to Jesus, "Excuse me, but this is my profession. I am an experienced fisherman, and I know that there are no fish in deep water, not a single one, and that fish are easier to catch at night than during the day." It's good to plan and think, but we must also leave room for God's grace to work in our lives. Only through the grace of God, along with human efforts, do miracles happen. When the Lord told Peter, "Put out into deep water," Peter said, "Master, we've worked hard all night and haven't caught anything. But

13 because you say so, I will let down the nets." This is obedient compliance with God, obedience that springs from faith. That's how Peter, after receiving the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, was able to win 3,000 people for the faith through a single sermon, leading them to be baptized. We give long sermons and advocate for the faith, but how many people can we ultimately win over to be baptized? Sometimes not a single person! When Christ said to Peter, "From now on you will be catching people," Peter obeyed and accepted the mission. "But where will I find people, Lord?" "At Pentecost when the Jews from all nations come to Jerusalem to celebrate. You only need to say a few words to them." Thus, 3,000 people not only came to the faith but also were baptized. All of this happened through God's grace. The Gospel of Luke teaches us that the word of God can be preached anywhere - by the shore of a lake, in a field, in a house, anywhere. We don't need large buildings or lecture halls or even magnificent places. Christ traveled around, did good, and healed everyone (Acts 10:38). By His grace, He made Peter, a simple and poor fisherman, one of the great pillars of the Church.

14 We also learn that for God, our small personal concerns are important. So, we pray, "God, just as You came to Peter to help him with fishing, come to us and help us. We need Your help for many things in our lives - at work, in our studies, in our families, in our church. We are sure that our private problems are important toYou. Just as You listened to the poor fishermen, You can also solve our problems." May the blessing of our Lord Christ, and the blessing of the miracle He performed for Peter, be bestowed upon us as well. To our triune God be the glory forever. Amen. Editor’s note: This sermon was selected for this volume about Bishop Michael because it reflects his concept of pastoral service: he followed in the footsteps of his Master, Jesus Christ. He traveled, did good, and prayed for the healing of all. He cared deeply about the personal problems of everyone in his community, no matter how small or serious those problems were.

15 Anba Michael as a spiritual father: Memories of a friend from the ecumenical community Prof. Dr. Reinhard Thöle Prof. Dr. R. Thöle bedankt sich bei Bischof Anba Michael für die Organisation eines mehrtägiges koptisch-evangelischen Seminars für seine Theologiestudenten im St.- Antonius-Kloster. Actually, one can't really write about this topic, especially when it concerns Bishop Michael St. Antonius (1942 - 2023). One can compile

16 impressions, encounters, and intuitions and immediately realize that all formulations fall short of expressing what one truly wants to convey. How can one do justice to the personality of this spiritual father or friend who, caught up in the whirlwind of heaven, held above the abyss of hell, radiated humility, love, confidence, and reliability? How can one describe the sense of home and security that his voice conveyed as an expression of his soul during the singing of prayers? How can one grasp what his monastic community mourns with tearful eyes under veils? How can one properly honor the fact that many Coptic Christians living in the European diaspora instinctively regard and venerate him as a saint? The Joy of the Monks The renewal and flourishing of Coptic monasticism, which began in the last century under Pope Cyril VI (1959-1971), sees the complete dedication of life to worship, the prayer of the heart, the monastic community, and the service of the Church in the world as an entry into the blessed dynamic of the kingdom of heaven in the tradition of the classical Coptic monastic fathers. The life of the Church and the world

17 should be anchored where the Triune God can be experienced in a secret way as He truly is. In 1980, two monks from the Baramous Monastery, including Father Michael, were sent to Germany to care for the growing Coptic communities in major cities and support the work initiated by Hegumen Salib Sourial. In this time of extreme diaspora, the goal was to help Copts who had left their geographical homeland for political reasons to maintain their spiritual home. The monks were flexible, mobile, and spiritually equipped for this task. They were the ideal spiritual servants for this situation. The longing for a Coptic center, which would now provide a local home for the newly emerging Coptic life, was fulfilled in 1980 when land was acquired in the Taunus region in Kröffelbach and a provisional chapel was set up on the first floor of what is now an auxiliary building. Step by step, what is felt today as if it has always existed came into being: the church consecrated by Pope Shenouda III in 1990, the cemetery, the guest houses, the monastery area, and the theology college. All of this, including setbacks, can only be true from the outside if it is true from the inside: in the sanctuary of the Church, in the laboratory of the monk's cell where heaven and hell battle for

18 the hearts of people, in fervent prayer during the services, and in the lived community. And the spiritual father, Anba Michael (ordained bishop in 2013), who is meant to lead, is sometimes like a big brother who looks down with joy at his younger siblings, encourages them to enter into the laws of the kingdom of heaven almost playfully. Joy in Christ should lead: mercy is stronger than reprimand, love is greater than weakness, patience is more important than impatience, humility is more formative than self- realization. And in the face of unresolved questions, the spiritual father points with a smiling finger upwards to God in the heights; that is his experience. In the monks' refectory, the atmosphere is light-hearted, like that of a sports team that can only play well when there is harmony within the team. And you can feel it in the monks' chapel, which becomes a prayer and energy event as soon as you enter, even before you can say a word of prayer. It is the gift of being touched, as the fathers of the prayer of the heart formulate it, and lingering at the threshold of the kingdom of God. Seeing into Hearts

19 In addition to celebrating liturgies and canonical hours, Anba Michael's spiritual care for individual believers was his charisma. He had the gift of seeing into hearts. That is, he instinctively encountered those who came to him for advice, intercession, and blessing and knew what was on their hearts and weighing them down. His humble and warmly loving manner made it easy for those entrusted to him to talk to him about difficult questions. He had the gift of seeing beyond the limits of time and place of the conversation and "knew" what was spiritually relevant "at that very hour" (Matthew 8:13).

20 He was not a talkative person, and he did not argue. His questions were genuine and unpretentious. He spoke simply, and his smile revealed that he was right. It is inappropriate to speak of the "success" of intercession when it has been granted in human estimation. One must speak of this area with caution. However, many of his spiritual children can testify to the helpful solutions to problems, including healings of illnesses, which they attribute to the intercession of their spiritual father. It seems as though the spiritual laws of the kingdom of heaven were so strong in him and in his environment that they could overwhelm earthly rules. Primacy of Prayer As a monk, abbot, and bishop, he had to bear a wealth of tasks, plans, finances, and decisions at the monastery, parish, and diocesan levels for decades. For him, the spiritual goal was always decisive, not quick success. It is natural that he experienced disappointments along the way. People who were there for some time, gladly accepted the blessings of the Coptic center, and then suddenly had to or wanted to go their own ways. For those who saw the life of the Church as

21 an environment to cook their own soup, he was uncomfortable because they felt exposed. However, he never condemned them or saw them as hopeless cases. Ecumenism, too, was primarily the fruit of prayer for him and not an intellectual product of theological conferences. One should remember the "Pro Oriente" conference in Kröffelbach and also a meeting of the "Philoxenia" circle, where, for the first time in Germany, Coptic, Ethiopian, Syrian, Syro-Indian, and Armenian clergy concelebrated at the Benedictine Monastery in Nütschau. His message is that when there is harmony through humility and dedication in the center of prayer between the churches, the traditional historical differences become secondary. Pope Tawadros II's statement during a visit to Rome this year could have also come from Anba Michael: "Despite the differences in our roots and affiliations, we are united by the love of Christ who dwells in us, and the multitude of our apostolic fathers and saints surrounds and guides us." Visitors to the services, especially those serving in the sanctuary of St. Antonius Monastery, will remember the image of Anba Michael, kneeling or sitting deep in prayer on a chair, and his zeal in

22 celebrating the liturgy as the center of life, standing at the altar. In Memoriam The liturgical remembrance of an exceptional spiritual father is an apostolic duty. In the Letter to the Hebrews, it says: “Remember those who have the rule over you, who have spoken unto you the Word of God. Follow their faith, considering the outcome of their manner of living." (Hebrews 13:7). It is also a sign of grateful love as required by the Fourth Commandment: "Honor your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12). This applies to the time after his earthly life when he will take his place at the heavenly altar. This elevates the relationship between a spiritual father and his disciples to a new level. The relationship is not annulled or weakened. The faithful remember him in prayer and worship at the earthly altar, and they are certain that the glorified spiritual father will also remember them at the heavenly altar. In the prayer of remembrance, the iconization of the memory of the faithful enters, as it were. Everything he was and did for them remains, concentrated, and this past remains in the present and the future as a clear image. The image that we now carry in faith of Anba Michael has become an

23 icon. We remember with love, sorrow, and joy the departed Bishop Anba Michel St. Antonius (1942- 2023) and hold fast to him as our extraordinary spiritual father and friend. It would be wrong to think that this concerns a private virtue or a personal ability of his. We hold fast to what he was and remains for us from God's perspective. Western theology would speak of "graced," and Eastern tradition would see him as enlightened by "divine energies." May his prayers and intercessions invoke God's holy name over us, so that His mercy may come upon us and His blessing may remain with us! © Benjamin Heeß

Senior Pastor Roland Fritsch Almost 24 hours a day in the service of God - Anba Michael's daily work program By Monk-Priest Raphael St. Anthony • In the time before his episcopal consecration, Father Michael used to get up at 3:30 a.m. He began his day by walking around the entire grounds of the four buildings, as well as the outer area of the monastery. He left the monastery through the main gate and went around the outside, and returned through the back side, near the cemetery. He then went to morning prayer at about 4:15 a.m. It was rare for any of the monks to be present at the liturgy before him. • After he became a bishop, this routine became somewhat shorter because his responsibilities had increased. After morning prayers, which

26 usually ended around 5:45 a.m., he took care of administrative work, such as processing invoices. On weekdays, he went to church, and Mass began at 6:45 a.m. After that, he might receive visitors or hear confessions, depending on what the congregation needed. He had breakfast around 11 or 12 o'clock and sometimes he would take a break around 1 or 2 o'clock in the afternoon. • At first, he didn't take any breaks; he was just taking a nap on the sofa in the guest area. In recent years, however, he began to take a

27 break in his cell for a few hours before coming down to Vespers (the Psalms) at 5 p.m., and he used to sit with the visitors. After that, he usually continued to work, e.g. sifting through the incoming mail. If there was work that fell within our area of activity, he would come to us to discuss it. He checked how the work was progressing, picking up the trash or tidying things up if necessary. When he noticed that I saw him collecting garbage, he would stop his work until I left. He often joined our work. For example, in 2011, when we were working in Building 4, he collected the empty water bottles and tea glasses, and disposed of the empty cement bags. • When he had no visitors, he used to have dinner with us monks after vespers. When visitors came to see him, he received them until about 9 p.m., and then he ate dinner. • At special times in the liturgical year, such as Passion Week or the fast of the Virgin Mary in the summer, when many people and groups from France or the Netherlands were visiting, he would talk to them after dinner around 8

28 p.m. and answer their questions. Sometimes he stayed with them until 11 or 12 p.m. He stayed until all the people had gone, and after his general session was over, those who confessed or needed his advice on a particular matter came in. And when he went to his cell, he didn't go to sleep right away. We saw the light in his cell on until 1:30 a.m. or 2:00 a.m. because he was still praying. Around 3:45 a.m. he attended the morning liturgy in the church. • If there were a lot of visitors, he was available all day to receive them. When there were no visitors, he began his administrative work. Sundays were exhausting for him, because after the morning liturgy, which ended around 5:30 a.m., he began to work until the time of Mass (9 a.m. - 12:30 p.m.). • He went to church about a quarter of an hour before the start of Mass. After Mass, he received visitors until vespers. After vespers, and after most of the guests had left, he had dinner and then retired to his cell. On weekdays, he didn't go to his cell until 10 p.m.,

29 and we could see that the light in his cell was still on until about midnight or 1 a.m. • Even if he was sick and it was a little difficult for him, he might skip the morning liturgy, but never the service. As long as he was in the monastery, he could not miss the service, even if he was sick. He put pressure on himself to celebrate Mass. • The last Epiphany feast he was very ill and could not go down the stairs from his cell. He was very moved and said to me: "This is the first time in more than forty years since I have been in the monastery that there is a Mass and I cannot attend." It was very stressful for him to be in the monastery and not be able to attend Mass. The Answered Prayer Anba Michael was always intently calling out to God for the well-being of his daughters and sons when they were sick or in distress. God often answered him because of his love, humility, and

30 purity of heart. His children rarely spoke of the miracles they experienced with him because they had grown accustomed to them. He never made anyone feel that he played a role in these miracles; instead, he always attributed them to God and the intercession of the saints. For non-Copts, belief in miracles is considered "outdated" and is not in line with modern science.
