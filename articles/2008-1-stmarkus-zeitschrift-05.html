The Human Rights of the Copts in Egypt

Ausgabe 1 · Seite 24

24 The Human Rights of the Copts in Egypt by Fouad Ibrahim, Wunstorf, Germany Published in: Thomas, M., Youssef, A.A., Gstrein, H., Strässle, P.M. (eds., 2006): Copts in Egypt – A Christian Minority under Siege. Zürich & Göttingen. pp. 142-148. Introduction Despite the fact that the Copts are the indigenous people of Egypt, they are denied the fundamental human right of practising their religion freely in their own country. In this paper, I shall give evidence of how the Copts, who constitute about 15 % of the country’s population, are suffering from religious discrimination, which is clearly against Article 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948 which Egypt signed, and which stipulates that “Everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion; this right includes freedom to change his religion or belief, and freedom, either alone or in community with others and in public or private, to manifest his religion or belief in teaching, practice, worship and observance.” Religious discrimination in Egypt is to be found both in the legislation and in the practices of the State administration, especially so among the police. In some cases, the legislation is seemingly in conformity with the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, but the behaviour of the executive authorities turns it to the opposite. The Egyptian constitution is full of contradictions and equivocations. For instance, it postulates religious freedom and the equality of all people, but at the same time it states that the shari’a, the Islamic law, is the main source of legislation. It is well known that according to the shari’a he who changes his religion is considered murtad, i.e. an apostate, and should be killed. According to the shari’a, too, a woman counts only as half a man both in inheritance and as a witness before a court of law. So, the declaration of equality of all people by the Egyptian constitution is a mere farce.

25 In the following, I shall give further evidence of the lack of religious freedom in Egypt: Construction and repair of church buildings The Egyptian Government still insists on applying the antiquated Hamayouni Decree of the 19th century which stipulates that a presidential decree is required for building a church. Repairing a toilet in a church is not allowed without the governor’s permission. In response to strong Coptic protest against the Ottoman decree of 1856, President Mubarak issued a decree in 1999 making the repair of churches and mosques alike subject to the Civil Construction Code of 1976. However, the practice of the executive authorities is strongly biased against church construction and repair. They always find ways for not fulfilling the already issued permits, as Sidhom (Watani 11/4/04: 1) documented. In the past fifty years, no church could be built in Asyut, although its Christian population increased by fourfold during that period. The State apparatus is playing a dishonest game: The President issues a permit to build a church in order to appear as tolerant vis-à-vis the outer world, but he knows very well that the local authorities will not allow it to be carried out; and he does not react to the complaints of the Copts who have been struggling for years with the local authorities about such issues. Why does it need a governor’s permit to repair a toilet? One of my personal acquaintances, at the time priest in Bahgoura in Upper Egypt, was held in detention for three days for repairing the broken door of a toilet on the church grounds without possessing a permit. We have never heard of things like this happening to an imam of a mosque. This is glaring inequality of treatment and a flagrant humiliation of all Copts in the country. It is a serious violation of Article 1 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948 which says: “All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights.” The Copts demand, therefore, the abolition of the Hamayouni Decree of 1856, and equality of treatment concerning churches and mosques. This equality should also be extended to cover the financial side. So far, the 82,000 mosques in Egypt are being built and maintained with money from the State treasury, and the salaries of the imams and other employees of the mosques are also paid from the State treasury, i.e. partly from taxes which were collected

26 from Copts. Churches and their employees must receive the same favourable treatment by the State. Freedom to choose one’s own religion Although conversion to Islam is strongly facilitated and even celebrated by the Egyptian authorities, Muslim converts to Christianity are subject to imprisonment and torture. Many instances of this have been made public over the Internet, especially the homepage of Copts.com. Those who are suspected to have helped in a conversion, are also brutally treated by the police. Especially sad are the cases of Christians who converted to Islam under pressure or blackmail, and later returned to Christianity. At present there are Muslim groups specialized in ensnaring poor Christian girls, deceiving them by promising presents and jobs, or by abducting and raping them. In the traditional societies of Upper Egypt, a girl that fell prey to rape, is a great shame for her family. So these girls have to accept whatever their kidnappers demand of them, and are thus quite easily converted to Islam. Also poor young men looking for employment fall prey to such groups. A Copt I know very well personally converted to Islam under massive economic pressure and three years later returned to his old Christian belief. In order to retrieve his original identity, he applied to the Administrative Court to have his now Muslim name changed again to his original Christian one. The Court refused his request. My friend applied to the State Council Administrative Court. That court, too, refused his appeal, sending him a letter in which the following reasons are stated: “- Article 2 of the Egyptian Constitution stipulates that Islam is the State religion and that the principles of the Islamic Sharia are a principal source of legislation. - Article 1 of the Civil Law stipulates that in the absence of applicable legislation customary law should be applied. If no applicable customary law exists, the judge has to apply the principles of the Islamic Sharia. In the present case, the following Sharia laws are applicable:  He who apostatizes from Islam shall die. (Quran)  Kill him who changes his religion. (Words of the Prophet Mohamed)

27 - The apostate has no right to marry, because he is considered dead, and a dead person is not in a position to marry. - Apostasy and marriage stand in contradiction to each other, because the apostate no longer exists (is dead). - Accordingly, the appellant is not allowed to change his name, for this is a consequence of his apostasy from Islam.” Copts demand that the Egyptian authorities should stop harassing individuals who change their religion, because it is their unalienable human right to choose their religion freely. “The right to manifest one’s own religion or belief in teaching, practice, worship and observance” (Article 18, Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948) There is an alarming increase of cases of Egyptian police harassing Christians who manifest their belief by harmless means, such as sticking the fish symbol on their own cars. On January 26, 2004, three young men were arrested and detained by the police for three months without fixing a court date, just because they had been carrying their bibles and other Christian literature in their baggage when they went on a holiday to Sinai. Copts demand that such unconstitutional harassment should be stopped and those who commit it be punished. Copts also demand the right to broadcast expressions of their belief to their people over the State-controlled TV channels and radio stations in proportional equality to what is granted to their Muslim fellow-citizens. And this not only for the simple reason that they, too, pay taxes and help finance these State media, but also for the reason that this is part of their religious human rights.

28 Campaigns against Copts in the State-controlled public media The Government must stop the campaigns against Christian citizens in the State- controlled TV and radio stations, newspapers, magazines and in the textbooks of schools. These are public media and should set an example for the media of the private sector in propagating tolerance and understanding instead of encouraging hatred and fanaticism. They should spread only proved facts about Christianity and not paint a dismal picture of it, whilst they disseminate only positive information about Islam. Religious discrimination by the State Religious discrimination is widespread in the State apparatus from the top to the bottom. Like corruption, it can only be stopped, if those who are responsible for it and commit it, are punished. Some steps to eradicate religious discrimination in this sphere are:  Removing the references to religious affiliation from the national identity cards, job applications, school and university enrolment forms etc., to avoid identification and discrimination of Christians.  Lifting restrictions concerning the enrolment of Christian students in certain State-run higher institutions, such as the police and the navy academies. Copts should be allowed to be enrolled in Al-Azhar University which is the largest university in the country and whose professors are paid from the taxes of Copts, too. While studying in that university is denied the native Copts, 15,000 Muslim foreigners were enrolled there mostly with Egyptian State scholarships in 2003 (Al-Ahram, 18/9/03: 14). Copts are also denied admission in the 7,570 Azhar institutes and colleges teaching 2.05 million students at the cost of Coptic and Muslim tax-payers (http://www.ahram.org.eg/Index.asp?CurFN=egyp1.htm&DID=8248 of 18/9/04).  Ensuring that Copts have an adequate share corresponding to their proportion of the Egyptian population – i.e. 15 % in all jobs that underlie State-control on all levels. Presently, the State is denying Copts key positions in the State administration. Sidhom (Watani 8/8/2004: 1) revealed concrete statistics proving this kind of State discrimination:

To keep up appearances, the President appoints a few Copts as Members of Parliament. Currently there are six such appointed members among the 454 members of Parliament. These can by no means be considered true representatives of the 10 million Copts of Egypt. They are like puppets on the President’s string and can even be harmful to the cause of the Copts. The Copts demand their adequate share of representation in the National Assembly. This could be achieved by reserving 15 % of the election districts for Coptic candidates only. This is possible in areas where Copts live in greater concentration, where their share can reach about 40 % of the total population.

30 Protection of Copts against terrorism Copts, their churches and their monasteries have been of late increasingly targets of terrorist assaults from the part of radical Islamic groups. This killing of innocent Christians and the damage done to their places of worship is being tolerated by the State security forces. None of the murderers of the massacre of El-Kosheh (Sohag) in 1999/2000, in which twenty-one Copts were killed, has been punished so far. The police themselves had tortured 1200 Copts in El-Kosheh one year earlier, thus giving green light for Islamists to assault the Copts. This culture of impunity, led by the security officers themselves, is on the advance, especially in Upper Egypt. In May 2004 a police officer killed Father Ibrahim Michael and two of his deacons in the Governorate of El-Minya, just because they had repaired the wall surrounding the church grounds. In February 2004, the son of the mayor of Telwana, Menufiya Governorate, killed the 19-year-old Ni’ma Malak, because she had taught Coptic children their religion at the village school. Instead of arresting the murderer, the police arrested the girl’s family and detained them for five days illegally. Meanwhile, the police looted and destroyed the houses of their relatives. The murderer is still at large and the Copts are being terrorized by the security authorities (La Revue Copte, No. 4-5, 2004: 1-3). Security authorities always manage to convince the courts of law that the killings were unintentional or that the murderers were lunatics. These examples show the flagrant violations of the religious human rights as laid down in the Declaration of 1948. State terrorism is so strong in Egypt that the church leaders inside the country do not dare to speak out openly about it for fear of repressions against their community. The absence of Coptic Church representatives in this very conference gives evidence of State intimidation.

31 The Copts appeal to State President Mubarak as the Father of the Nation to put an end to police terrorism against Copts to protect the lives and the property of Copts to return to the Coptic Church its property (waqf) to abolish the Hamayouni Decree, lift all restrictions on the construction and repair of churches and punish executives who impose unfair restrictions to stop the discrimination against Copts in the appointment to higher State positions and in the enrolment in higher State institutions to grant the Copts their fair share of representation in the National Assembly and above all, to reform the constitution as well as the legislation, cleansing them from all offences against the Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948, thus finally ensuring legal equality of all Egyptians and allowing for religious freedom for all in the country.
